\begin{abstract}
Computer vision is increasingly used to automate waste sorting, but models are
typically trained on clean, curated datasets and degrade sharply when deployed in
the field, where lighting, clutter, and occlusion differ from training data. This
work studies that lab-to-field gap across three tasks (classification, detection,
and segmentation) and two model paradigms (task-specific specialists and
foundation models). We first benchmark specialist and zero-shot foundation models
under matched in-domain and cross-domain conditions, then fine-tune a single
vision-language foundation model with low-rank adapters into one unified model
that performs all three tasks. Across seven cross-domain benchmarks spanning five
distinct waste domains, the unified model is the best, or statistically tied for
best, on every benchmark, using one model and a lightweight adapter rather than
many specialist pipelines. Specialist models retain the highest in-domain
accuracy but collapse under domain shift (a relative drop of 58 to 80 percent),
while the unified model trades a small amount of in-domain accuracy for markedly
more robust transfer. Because one model with a lightweight adapter replaces
several task-specific pipelines, the approach is also simpler and cheaper to
deploy in the field.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Rapid urbanization has increased the volume of solid waste, and efficient sorting
is central to recycling and resource recovery. Computer vision can automate three
levels of waste understanding: image-level classification of material type,
object-level detection of waste items, and pixel-level segmentation of waste
regions. In practice, however, models trained on curated laboratory datasets
often fail when deployed in the field, where scenes are cluttered, lighting
varies, and objects are occluded. High benchmark accuracy therefore does not
guarantee reliable deployment; this discrepancy is the lab-to-field gap.

Two paradigms address waste vision. Task-specific models are trained for a single
task on domain-specific data and optimize peak accuracy in controlled conditions.
Foundation models are pre-trained on large, diverse corpora and can be adapted to
many tasks, trading some peak accuracy for broader generalization. Deploying a
separate specialist per task is also operationally costly: it multiplies memory,
maintenance, and integration overhead.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we benchmark specialist and
foundation models for classification, detection, and segmentation under matched
in-domain and cross-domain protocols, quantifying the lab-to-field gap. Second,
we fine-tune a single vision-language foundation model, Florence-2, with low-rank
adapters into one unified model that performs all three tasks, evaluated on seven
cross-domain benchmarks across five domains. Third, we package the unified model
as an interactive single-model demo that serves classification, detection, and
segmentation from one inference pipeline, illustrating its deployment advantage
over a stack of task-specific models.

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Task-specific vision models.} Convolutional and transformer classifiers
such as ResNet \cite{resnet}, EfficientNet \cite{efficientnet}, and the Vision
Transformer \cite{vit} achieve strong supervised accuracy. Detectors such as
YOLOv8 \cite{yolov8} and Faster R-CNN \cite{fasterrcnn}, and segmentation models
such as U-Net \cite{unet}, DeepLabV3+ \cite{deeplab}, and Mask R-CNN
\cite{maskrcnn}, are similarly accurate in-domain but are known to degrade under
distribution shift.

\textbf{Foundation models.} CLIP \cite{clip} aligns image and text encoders for
zero-shot classification; Grounding DINO \cite{gdino} performs open-set,
text-prompted detection; and the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{sam} produces
class-agnostic masks from prompts. Florence-2 \cite{florence2} unifies
captioning, detection, grounding, and segmentation in a single
encoder-decoder model driven by task prompts. We adapt Florence-2 with LoRA
\cite{lora} via the PEFT library \cite{peft}, which inserts small trainable
matrices while freezing the backbone.

\textbf{Waste datasets.} We use TrashNet \cite{trashnet} and TACO \cite{taco} as
curated training sources, and RealWaste \cite{realwaste}, Trash-ICRA19
\cite{icra19}, the Dense Waste Segmentation Dataset (DWSD), ZeroWaste-f
\cite{zerowaste}, and WaRP \cite{warp} as cross-domain test sets. Prior work on
foundation models for waste classification \cite{babe} motivates studying their
generalization, which we extend to detection and segmentation and to a single
unified model.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
For each task we distinguish two evaluation regimes. \emph{In-domain}: train and
test come from the same dataset and distribution. \emph{Cross-domain}: the model
is evaluated on a held-out dataset from a different domain, never seen during
training. The cross-domain regime measures field robustness and is the focus of
this work.

\subsection{Stage 1: Specialist and Foundation Baselines}
We train task-specific models on the curated source for each task and evaluate
them in-domain and cross-domain. For classification we use ViT-Base, ResNet-50,
and EfficientNet-B0 trained on TrashNet; for detection, YOLOv8m and Faster R-CNN
trained on TACO; for segmentation, DeepLabV3+, U-Net, and Mask R-CNN trained on
TACO masks. As foundation baselines we evaluate CLIP (classification), Grounding
DINO (detection), and SAM (segmentation) zero-shot.

\subsection{Stage 2: Unified Florence-2 with LoRA}
We fine-tune Florence-2-large with LoRA into a single model that performs all
three tasks through task prompts: \texttt{<CAPTION>} for classification,
\texttt{<OD>} for detection, and a referring-expression prompt for segmentation,
with segmentation polygons quantized to location tokens. Training data combine
TrashNet classification, TACO detection, and TACO polygon segmentation into one
shuffled multi-task corpus. LoRA is applied to the attention projection layers
(rank 16, alpha 32, dropout 0.05) and trained for three epochs. Crucially, none
of the cross-domain test datasets are used in training.

\subsection{Inference Methods for the Unified Model}
Because the evaluation metrics are class-agnostic, we use the Florence-2 head best
matched to each metric, applied uniformly across all datasets of a task. For
detection we use the class-agnostic region-proposal head, which recovers recall
on dense scenes where the labelled detector under-detects small objects. For
segmentation we use a multi-instance cascade: phrase-grounding locates every
waste instance, per-region segmentation produces a mask for each, and the masks
are unioned into a binary foreground.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\textbf{Datasets.} Classification: TrashNet (in-domain), RealWaste and WaRP-C
(cross-domain). Detection: TACO (in-domain), Trash-ICRA19, ZeroWaste-f, and WaRP-D
(cross-domain). Segmentation: TACO masks (in-domain), DWSD and ZeroWaste-f
(cross-domain). The five cross-domain domains are landfill, underwater,
recycling-plant, cluttered-conveyor, and dense-scene waste.

\textbf{Metrics.} Classification uses accuracy and macro-F1. Detection uses
class-agnostic precision, recall, and F1 at an intersection-over-union (IoU)
threshold of 0.5. Segmentation uses binary mean IoU (mIoU). All models within a
task are scored with the identical metric to ensure a fair comparison.

\textbf{Implementation.} All experiments run on an NVIDIA A100-40GB GPU with
PyTorch 2.0.1, Transformers 4.40.2, and PEFT 0.10.0. Cross-domain datasets are
evaluated on their held-out test splits only.

\subsection{Detection}
YOLOv8m leads in-domain (0.641 F1) but falls to 0.13--0.22 cross-domain --- a
relative drop of roughly 65--78\%. The contrast is sharpest on underwater debris
(Trash-ICRA19), where YOLOv8 reaches only 0.139 F1 while the open-vocabulary
Grounding DINO, which was never trained on TACO, reaches 0.372; broad pre-training
clearly helps when the domain shifts. The unified model is the best on all three
cross-domain detection sets (0.505, 0.272, 0.281), combining the foundation
models' robustness with task-adapted accuracy.

\subsection{Segmentation}
Specialist segmenters collapse cross-domain; DeepLabV3+ falls from 0.454 to 0.048
on DWSD. The unified model wins DWSD (0.180) and is statistically tied with the
best specialist, Mask R-CNN, on ZeroWaste-f (0.160 vs 0.169). A paired Wilcoxon
signed-rank test gives $p{=}0.98$ and the bootstrap 95\% confidence interval of
the per-image difference includes zero, so the two are indistinguishable.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Summary}
Aggregating across the seven cross-domain benchmarks, the unified Florence-2 is
the best, or statistically tied for best, on all seven: it wins all
classification and detection benchmarks and DWSD segmentation, and ties on
ZeroWaste-f segmentation. Notably, ZeroWaste-f is the one cross-domain benchmark
where a specialist does not collapse (Mask R-CNN 0.169 vs 0.084 on DWSD), which
makes the parity there meaningful rather than a low-signal corner case.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}
\textbf{Accuracy-generalization trade-off.} Specialists peak in-domain but lose
56--74 accuracy points (classification) and most of their detection and
segmentation quality under shift. The unified model gives up a little in-domain
accuracy for broadly stable transfer, which is the property that matters for
deployment. Fig.~\ref{fig:retain} makes this concrete: we define cross-domain
\emph{retention} as the mean cross-domain score divided by the in-domain score.
The best specialist per task retains only 20--32\% of its in-domain quality,
whereas the unified model retains 69--96\%.

\textbf{One model versus many.} Deploying a separate specialist per task is costly
in practice: holding several models in memory multiplies GPU footprint, routing
inputs between them adds latency and orchestration logic, and each model carries
its own training and maintenance pipeline. Our study spans nine task-specific and
foundation baselines; a production system built from them would need to load and
maintain several distinct networks. The unified model replaces that stack with a
single backbone and one lightweight LoRA adapter of only a few tens of megabytes,
serving classification, detection, and segmentation by changing the text prompt
rather than the model. This consolidation --- many models reduced to one --- is the
practical payoff of the unified design and is what makes it deployable at scale.

\textbf{Why foundation models generalize.} The specialist collapse and the
unified model's robustness follow from how each was trained. A specialist learns
features tuned to one curated distribution; when the test distribution shifts ---
new backgrounds, lighting, object scales, and clutter --- those features no longer
apply, and in-domain accuracies of 80--96\% fall to as low as 17--40\%, a
relative drop of roughly 58--80\%. Detection and segmentation specialists show the
same pattern: YOLOv8 falls from 0.641 to 0.14--0.22 F1, and DeepLabV3+ from 0.454
to 0.05--0.13 mIoU. Zero-shot foundation models resist this collapse because they
were pre-trained on large, diverse corpora, but their in-domain ceiling is lower
(for instance CLIP reaches only 67.8\% on TrashNet). The unified Florence-2
combines both strengths: the LoRA adapter specialises a broad vision-language
representation to waste while leaving its general robustness intact, so the model
is rarely the single best in-domain yet is consistently strong across unseen
domains.

\textbf{Practical recommendations.} Two guidelines follow for real-world systems.
When the deployment environment is controlled and matches the training data ---
for example a sorting line with fixed lighting and a known object set --- a
task-specific model gives the highest accuracy at low inference cost. When the
environment is open and variable, as in street-level collection or surveillance,
the unified foundation model is preferable: it degrades far less under shift and
covers all three tasks from one model. In short, expected field conditions, not
in-domain benchmark accuracy, should drive model choice for waste vision.

\section{Demo and Deployment}
To make the operational benefit concrete and demonstrate single-model deployment,
we built ``Playground,'' a web application (FastAPI backend, React frontend) that
serves the unified Florence-2 model for all supported tasks through one inference
endpoint. A user uploads a field image and selects a task group ---
captioning/classification, detection, grounding, segmentation, OCR, or a cascaded
multi-instance mode --- and the same model and adapter produce the output, with no
model switching or per-task pipeline. The interface reports inference latency and
the raw structured output and supports exporting the annotated result. This
illustrates that the unified model is directly deployable as one service, in
contrast to orchestrating several task-specific models.

Figs.~\ref{fig:pg-cls}--\ref{fig:pg-seg} show the unified model performing
classification, detection, and segmentation on the same cluttered, street-level
waste image, each produced by the same model and adapter.

\section{Limitations}
Florence-2 segmentation emits polygons as discrete tokens and is therefore lossy
on dense scenes, which is why a strong instance segmenter remains competitive on
ZeroWaste-f. Several cross-domain baselines (RealWaste, Trash-ICRA19, DWSD) are
carried from a prior matched evaluation, and the in-domain detection F1 for
specialists is reported on the same protocol. Finally, results are from single
training runs; multiple seeds would tighten the cross-domain estimates.

\section{Conclusion}
We studied the lab-to-field gap in waste vision across classification, detection,
and segmentation, comparing task-specific and foundation models. A single
Florence-2 model fine-tuned with lightweight low-rank adapters is the best, or
statistically tied for best, on all seven cross-domain benchmarks across five
domains, while replacing many specialist pipelines with one model. Specialists
peak in-domain but lose 58 to 80 percent of their quality under domain shift,
whereas the unified model retains far more and remains preferable wherever
deployment conditions vary. Specialists remain the better choice only when the
deployment environment exactly matches training. For real-world waste systems,
where input conditions vary, a unified foundation model with lightweight
fine-tuning is the more robust and more practical choice.
